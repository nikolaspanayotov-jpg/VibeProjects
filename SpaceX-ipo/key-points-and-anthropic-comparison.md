# SpaceX IPO with hindsight—and what it teaches us about Anthropic

## Main finding

**SpaceX's trading reflects a changing balance between exceptional growth expectations, a scarce initial float, the cash required to deliver that growth, and a widening supply of tradable shares.** Its operating business improved markedly during a period in which its shares fell sharply from their peak. Buying the business and choosing an entry valuation were different decisions.

The most relevant parallel with Anthropic is the AI infrastructure investment cycle. The listed SpaceX includes **rockets, Starlink, xAI/Grok, X, and—since August—Cursor**. It also sells substantial compute capacity to Anthropic. This makes the comparison closer than “space company versus AI company” suggests, while introducing shared customer, supplier, financing, and demand risks. Sources: [SpaceX Q3 filing][q3] and [Anthropic S-1][anthropic].

This review uses SpaceX's final IPO prospectus, Q2 and Q3 disclosures, dated market reporting, and price observations through the **October 29, 2026 regular-session close**. Company disclosures, reported explanations, and analytical inferences are distinguished below. Selected prices are observations, not a complete daily series or proof of causation. The related [Anthropic summary](../Anthropic-ipo/key-points-summary.md) remains a separate file.

## What affected each phase of trading?

### 1. The initial surge: scarcity, attention, and expectations

SpaceX sold approximately **638.9 million new shares**, including the full underwriter option, at $135, generating **$85.675 billion of net proceeds**. Despite the large dollar amount, the initial shares available for public trading represented less than 5% of the company. A large IPO can still have a small float relative to total ownership. [Sources: Q2 filing, Note 1; contemporaneous Reuters reporting.][q2]

The combination of a widely followed company, limited immediately tradable shares, and ambitious expectations made the price sensitive to marginal buying. BMLL's market-data review found expensive short-dated upside options and estimated substantial passive buying associated with rapid index additions. Nasdaq confirmed entry into the Nasdaq-100 effective July 7. These were flow and positioning considerations, distinct from changes in the present value of operating cash flows. [Sources: BMLL; Nasdaq index announcement.][bmll]

**Interpretation:** the early price discovery included a scarcity premium and strong expectations. The $225.64 print did not establish that the business had permanently earned that valuation. Options activity can amplify volatility, but the reviewed data do not establish the direction of dealers' net hedging or prove a particular “gamma squeeze.”

### 2. The June–July retreat: the valuation and funding test

By July 15, contemporaneous reporting pointed to valuation reassessment, profit-taking, concerns about debt-funded AI expansion, and interest-rate sensitivity. The coming earnings report and lock-up releases added uncertainty. This describes the concerns being reported at the time; it does not quantify each concern's contribution to the decline. [Source: Reuters, July 15.][july-reuters]

Several risks were visible before the IPO: the prospectus showed **2025 revenue of $18.674 billion**, **$2.589 billion of operating losses**, and **$4.937 billion of net losses**. Its historical results had been retrospectively combined with xAI and X because of common control. Investors were purchasing a capital-intensive combined group with established and developing businesses, rather than a stand-alone profitable rocket operator. [Source: prospectus, basis of presentation and summary financial data.][prospectus]

**Interpretation:** when valuation gives substantial credit for future scale, a change in required returns, execution confidence, or expected funding needs can outweigh continued revenue growth. The decline does not require the conclusion that Starlink or launch technology suddenly stopped working.

### 3. Early August: strong growth met a much larger cash requirement

Q2 revenue reached **$7.814 billion**, up approximately **92%** year over year, and adjusted EBITDA was **$3.538 billion**. However, the group still recorded **$143 million of operating losses** and **$541 million of net losses**, while quarterly capex was **$18.369 billion**. AI accounted for **$15.828 billion** of that capex and produced a **$1.257 billion operating loss**. [Sources: Q2 filing; Q3 release's comparative tables.][q2]

Contemporaneous reporting emphasized questions about AI returns and the ability of profitable Starlink to support the expansion. The positive revenue picture did not remove concern over how much capital was needed, when it would pay back, and which investors would finance it. [Source: Reuters on the first earnings reaction.][august-reuters]

**Interpretation:** “revenue beat expectations” was insufficient on its own. Investors also had to assess cash conversion, infrastructure utilization, depreciation, financing terms, and future equity supply. This is one of the strongest lessons for Anthropic's first public results.

### 4. The first unlock: anticipation and actual selling were different

The prospectus allowed up to **911.5 million shares** to become eligible after Q2 earnings, adding to approximately 639 million shares sold in the IPO. That could more than double the initial tradable supply. Yet the stock **rose 6.1% on August 6** after the preceding day's earnings-related fall. [Sources: prospectus, Shares Eligible for Future Sale; AP's dated report.][unlock-ap]

**Interpretation:** investors can price in expected selling before restrictions expire; actual sales may be smaller than the eligible amount, or demand may absorb them. A relief rally or repositioning is plausible, but price action alone does not establish how much any holder sold or how much short covering occurred. The evidence refutes a mechanical rule that an unlock must cause a same-day fall.

### 5. The recovery: better evidence for monetization

The recovery into autumn occurred as AI infrastructure contracts and the prospect of monetizing new compute became more prominent. By Q3, SpaceX had **$14.575 billion of revenue**, **$2.791 billion of operating income**, and profitable operations in its AI/SI segment. Connectivity continued to grow strongly, and Starship completed its first revenue-generating mission carrying Starlink V3 satellites. Cursor also entered the reported group in August. [Sources: Q3 release; Q3 filing.][q3-release]

**Interpretation:** these developments supplied evidence for the long-term case and help explain renewed interest. They do not prove that every rally day had the same cause. Cursor contributed **$608 million of Q3 revenue**, so all reported growth should not be characterized as organic growth in Grok demand. Much of SI's acceleration came from selling cloud capacity.

### 6. October: earnings strength, more supply, and renewed AI risk sensitivity

After October 20 results and discussion of a possible Tesla combination, the shares rose to $159.31 on October 21, then fell to $144.75 on October 22. The latter session coincided with the prospectus's earnings-linked release of up to approximately **1.3 billion shares** and a market-wide sell-off in which the Nasdaq Composite fell **2.6%**. [Sources: price history; prospectus; Reuters market report.][october-market]

Management also guided on its Q3 call to **$35–40 billion of Q4 capex**, versus $19.593 billion in Q3, and said Q4 gross margins should be more consistent with Q2 after Q3 benefited from one-time items and mix. On October 26, the companies announced a **binding commitment** for approximately **$40 billion of GPU finance leasing** through an Apollo-managed entity. Closing conditions remained; this was not $40 billion of unrestricted cash already added to the September balance sheet. Sources: [earnings-call transcript][q3-call] and [Apollo announcement][apollo].

The latest concentration story adds another layer: SpaceX disclosed that one SI customer generated **34.5% of Q3 revenue**; Bloomberg identified that customer as Anthropic on October 29. That identification is reported sourcing, whereas the percentage is in the filing. It highlights a risk in the recent valuation debate, but the report cannot explain the earlier June–August decline. Sources: [Q3 filing, Note 3][q3] and [Bloomberg][customer-report].

**Interpretation:** the roughly $140–170 area reflects continuing disagreement about growth, spending, concentration, dilution, and market conditions. It is not evidence of a stable fair-value band. The simultaneous earnings, unlock, and market events also prevent a clean single-cause explanation for October 22.

## The most important comparison: Anthropic is a customer, competitor, and shared risk exposure

Anthropic's S-1 describes SpaceXAI agreements with **up to $120.5 billion of spending through terms extending into 2029**, reaching approximately **$3.1 billion per month when all capacity is delivered**, expected in December 2026. After initial non-cancelable periods ending in January and March 2027, either party can cancel the respective agreements on **90 days' notice**. This is not a guaranteed, non-cancelable $120.5 billion revenue backlog for SpaceX. [Source: Anthropic MD&A, Infrastructure Service Arrangements.][anthropic-mda]

SpaceX's Q3 filing independently discloses the 34.5% single-customer concentration in its SI segment. **Calculated:** that percentage of $14.575 billion equals approximately **$5.03 billion of Q3 group revenue**, or $1.68 billion per month averaged over the quarter. Bloomberg's identification of Customer B as Anthropic should remain explicitly attributed. The Q3 average is not the same as Anthropic's later fully delivered monthly commitment. Sources: [Q3 Note 3][q3] and [Bloomberg, October 29][customer-report].

The implications run in both directions:

- **Anthropic's usage and funding success can support SpaceX's compute revenue.** Strong enterprise demand for Claude can drive the need for more capacity.
- **Expensive scarce capacity benefits the supplier's revenue but pressures the customer's costs.** Cheaper capacity can help Anthropic while putting pressure on SpaceX's rental economics; the net effect depends on volume, costs, utilization, and contract terms.
- **A disruption can propagate through the relationship.** Anthropic may face capacity risk; SpaceX may face utilization and renewal risk if customer demand changes.
- **They compete above the infrastructure layer.** Grok and Cursor compete in AI models, agents, and coding workflows, even while SpaceX supplies compute to Anthropic.
- **Owning both may provide less diversification than the business labels suggest.** Shared exposure to AI spending and financing is real; the degree of future stock-return correlation is not established by this short history.

These are economic inferences from the disclosed relationship. Supplier revenue and customer spending are legitimate separate company transactions, but adding both companies' revenues does not measure distinct end-customer AI demand without adjusting for transactions within that supply chain.

## What to apply to Anthropic's IPO

1. **Value the business at the price actually available to you.** SpaceX's allocation price, first trade, and early peak were different valuations. Recalculate Anthropic's market capitalization at an aftermarket entry, rather than carrying forward the offer-price investment case after a large opening jump.
2. **Treat early scarcity as temporary.** Get the initial float, fully diluted economic share count, holder-specific release schedule, and registered resale rights. Anthropic already discloses first-day and staged releases; “wait 180 days” would miss important supply changes.
3. **Do not treat an unlock as a directional forecast.** SpaceX's first major unlock day rose after substantial anticipatory weakness. Model the size relative to float, investor positioning, trading volume, and likely sellers; distinguish eligibility from actual sales.
4. **Expect spending guidance to compete with revenue headlines.** For Anthropic, track expensive short-term compute, new-capacity cost steps, utilization, cash collateral, capex, and lease payments alongside usage growth. Management's existing margin-compression warning deserves weight before the first earnings report.
5. **Normalize profit measures.** SpaceX's EBITDA excludes substantial asset costs; Anthropic's net loss includes large fair-value adjustments and IPO-triggered compensation can distort comparisons. Reconcile both to operating economics and cash obligations without dismissing dilution as economically irrelevant.
6. **Test revenue quality and concentration.** For Anthropic, distinguish run-rate annualization from GAAP revenue or binding commitments, examine the unusual retention cohort, and track the disclosed 5%–7% policy-violating revenue estimate. For SpaceX, do not interpret compute rentals as proof that its own model has won the AI market.
7. **Assess the shared capital cycle.** Anthropic's ability to fund capacity purchases matters to suppliers such as SpaceX; supplier pricing and reliability matter to Anthropic's margins and service. Analyze a demand/financing slowdown across the relationship.
8. **Look for evidence that changes the valuation case.** Durable expansion, successful capacity delivery, improving cost per useful task, and cash conversion support value. Another large funding or compute headline alone does not establish an attractive investment return.
9. **Separate known risks from later information.** Capital intensity, losses, control, and phased lock-ups were visible in SpaceX's prospectus. Q2 spending, Q3 customer concentration, Cursor's contribution, and the possible Tesla transaction supplied later information. Apply the same discipline to Anthropic instead of retroactively assuming every price move was predictable.
10. **Use scenarios, not SpaceX's chart as a forecast.** A “pop, crash, recovery” sequence is one realized outcome. Anthropic could trade differently depending on offer valuation, market conditions, float, results, and new information. There is no evidence here for a rule to buy on day one, after a fixed percentage fall, or on an unlock date.

These are analytical lessons grounded in the disclosed mechanisms and observed sequence, not a buy/sell recommendation or an Anthropic price target.

## A practical valuation discipline for both

Use the same questions but different operating models:

- **What must be true at the entry valuation?** Identify the growth, normalized margin, reinvestment, and terminal assumptions needed to justify it.
- **Which cash flows are proven and which remain prospective?** For SpaceX, separate connectivity, launch, compute rentals, and model/application earnings. For Anthropic, separate existing usage economics from future model and market opportunities.
- **How much capital is still required?** Include debt, equipment leases, fixed capacity commitments, replacement needs, and adverse funding terms. Cash balances and announcements of financing commitments are not interchangeable.
- **How much value accrues to each economic share?** Incorporate awards, notes, acquisition shares, and relevant benefit-fund distributions; do not double-count conversions of existing economic shares.
- **What is already expected by the market?** The surprise in revenue, margin, spending, or financing often matters more than the absolute headline.

For scale only, **calculated** from SpaceX's October 15 reported Class A/B shares and the October 29 close, basic equity market value was approximately **$1.95 trillion**. Calculated TTM revenue through September was **$32.043 billion**: FY2025 $18.674bn − 9M2025 $13.714bn + 9M2026 $27.083bn. That implies approximately **61 times trailing sales**, before dilution, net-debt/lease adjustments, or forward growth. This is a historical-sales reference, not an enterprise-value multiple, fair-value conclusion, or appropriate multiple to apply to Anthropic. [Sources: prospectus; Q3 filing; price history.][q3]
