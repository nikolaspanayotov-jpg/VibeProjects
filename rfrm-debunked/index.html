What the evidence labels mean

Each label describes the argument being made. It is not an automatic true-or-false verdict on the whole proposal. “Legal obstacle” identifies a compatibility question; it does not claim a court has found the policy unlawful.

Internal contradiction
Two claims in the plan cannot both hold as stated.
Legal obstacle
A potential conflict with legal or treaty obligations needs to be resolved.
Costing uncertainty
Assumptions, behaviour or delivery risks prevent confidence in the claimed result.
Strong evidence
Direct evidence supports the specific counterargument, such as the existing legal baseline.
Distributional trade-off
The policy can produce benefits while shifting costs or risks to others.
Reasonable policy with caveats

Approve Rosebank immediately

Original measure 1 in Unlocking the North Sea. PDF p. 24

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Approve the Rosebank North Sea oil and gas project immediately.

Rosebank could support jobs and production, but immediate approval offers no guaranteed household price cut and must address the consenting evidence.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Approve Rosebank immediately

Rosebank could sustain offshore employment, engineering contracts and tax receipts, so its economic benefits deserve examination. But approving a field is different from delivering cheaper fuel. Its output would be predominantly oil, while the household heating problem is largely about gas. International trading also means domestic extraction does not automatically create a discounted British price. An immediate political commitment risks prejudging the environmental assessment rather than resolving it. The Commons Library records that the earlier consent was effectively quashed in January 2025 and fresh applications followed in July 2026.

A ministerial answer on 14 September 2026 said the regulatory process was continuing; that dated answer does not establish the project's final status today. A credible approval case should therefore publish the assessment, realistic production timetable, public revenue implications and conditions protecting the environment. Workers and supply chains need a durable decision that survives scrutiny, alongside investment beyond the lifetime of one field. [1] [2] [3]

02

Restart licensing for new oil and gas fields

Original measure 2 in Unlocking the North Sea. PDF p. 24

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Resume issuing exploration licences for new oil and gas fields.

New licences can encourage exploration, but they cannot guarantee discoveries, rapid production or insulation from international fuel prices.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Restart licensing for new oil and gas fields

A predictable licensing regime can support exploration businesses and preserve specialist skills. There is a legitimate debate about how much of Britain's continuing fuel demand should be supplied domestically. However, a licence is only an early step: commercially viable discoveries, finance, infrastructure and development consent must follow before production. That makes licensing a weak answer to an immediate cost of living crisis. The North Sea is also a mature basin, so ministers should distinguish slowing decline from reversing it.

The Commons Library's September 2026 briefing describes the government's policy against new licences while allowing existing licensed projects to seek consent. Restarting rounds would therefore need a clear account of what additional investment is genuinely caused by the change. The trade-off is between possible future jobs and receipts, environmental impacts and capital committed to long-lived assets. Judging projects against realistic demand and price scenarios would be more informative than counting licences as energy delivered. [1] [2]

03

Legalise onshore exploration in new licences

Original measure 3 in Unlocking the North Sea. PDF p. 24

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Allow onshore oil and gas exploration through new licences.

Licensing onshore exploration does not settle local planning, environmental permissions or the separate restrictions on shale fracking.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Legalise onshore exploration in new licences

Domestic onshore exploration could create some local contracts and identify useful resources. The proposal nevertheless needs much more precision about geography and drilling methods. Conventional exploration and high-volume hydraulic fracturing are different activities, and issuing a licence would not by itself resolve the permissions needed to drill or produce. A ministerial answer on 16 October 2026 confirmed that the effective shale-fracking moratorium continued for existing licences and distinguished conventional low-volume techniques. The same answer described forthcoming legislation, rather than establishing an already operative blanket statutory ban.

Nearby residents would need clear protections for water, traffic, noise and site restoration, together with a meaningful planning process. Commercial success is uncertain, so promises of large tax receipts or cheap gas should not be booked in advance. Any assessment should compare the expected recoverable resource and development time with the disruption borne by host communities, rather than assuming permission to explore guarantees a productive industry. 04

Abolish the Energy Profits Levy immediately

Original measure 4 in Unlocking the North Sea. PDF p. 24

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Immediately remove the additional tax on oil and gas profits known as the Energy Profits Levy.

Removing the levy could improve investment incentives, but gives up public revenue without requiring firms to invest or cut British energy prices.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Abolish the Energy Profits Levy immediately

Frequent tax changes can discourage investment, and removing an additional profit tax could make some marginal developments more attractive. That is a real potential benefit for offshore workers and suppliers. The fiscal question is how much extra activity the tax cut would generate compared with the revenue lost on production that would happen anyway. A Treasury answer in July 2026 put the levy at 38%, alongside a 40% permanent regime, with reliefs affecting actual liabilities. Abolition therefore benefits existing profitable operations as well as possible new investment.

Nothing in this measure requires recipients to spend the saving in Britain or sell fuel below market prices. HMRC has also published proposals for a different, price-triggered replacement after the levy ends; that proposal should not be confused with an existing additional charge. A credible costing needs investment responses, timing and the alternative use of lost revenue, rather than treating a producer tax cut as an automatic consumer saving. [1] [2] [3]

Change the North Sea regulator's objective and name

Original measure 5 in Unlocking the North Sea. PDF p. 24

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Restore maximum economic extraction as the regulator's statutory objective, remove its Net Zero obligation and rename the North Sea Transition Authority the Oil & Gas Authority.

A new name and narrower objective cannot remove depletion or commercial constraints, while dropping emissions duties weakens accountability for production impacts.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Change the North Sea regulator's objective and name

A clearer regulatory mandate could help operators understand how investment proposals will be judged. But the premise needs care: the regulator's published strategy already combines maximising economic recovery with a duty to assist the net zero target. Economic recovery has therefore not simply disappeared from its framework. Removing the emissions element would change how competing costs and benefits are weighed, rather than uncovering resources by administrative instruction. The measure also needs to explain how the regulator would treat methane leaks, platform energy use and investment that reduces operational emissions.

These affect the public interest even where oil and gas remain necessary. A focus on extraction could favour continued production, but cannot make every discovery commercially viable or replace depleted reservoirs. Parliament should assess the proposed statutory wording, the treatment of emissions and the consequences for taxpayers and offshore workers. Renaming the organisation provides little evidence of additional output or lower bills on its own. [1] [2]

Abolish the Emissions Trading Scheme

Original measure 6 in Reindustrialising Britain. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Abolish the UK Emissions Trading Scheme.

Abolition can lower some domestic carbon costs while increasing the carbon-border exposure of covered British exports to the EU.

What this depends on
  • Stop implementing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism: The UK import charge is a different instrument from the EU border regime faced by British exports.
  • Cut household energy bills by £250: The household saving depends on electricity-price effects and pass-through.
Analysis and sources: analysis of Abolish the Emissions Trading Scheme

Carbon charges are a real cost for energy-intensive businesses, and removing them could relieve some immediate pressure. But the relevant comparison includes trade, investment and public revenue, rather than the domestic bill alone. The EU’s definitive carbon-border regime began on 1 January 2026. Its importers can deduct a verified carbon price paid abroad, subject to the scheme’s rules. Removing the UK ETS could therefore increase the charge associated with covered British exports, including qualifying steel, aluminium and cement products.

The legal certificate obligation sits with the EU importer, but British suppliers can face commercial consequences through prices, margins or demand. The effect varies with product coverage, emissions, reliefs and phasing; it is not a uniform charge on all UK exports. Current UK–EU linkage negotiations offer a possible exemption route, contingent on agreement. Abolition would also remove an emissions incentive and weaken the value of earlier clean investment. Some carbon revenue could shift from the UK to the EU. A larger gross border charge is not automatically an equal increase in combined production and border costs, because domestic carbon costs may fall. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Stop implementing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

Original measure 7 in Reindustrialising Britain. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Stop implementing the UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, including the proposed charge affecting fertiliser imports.

Cancelling the UK import mechanism does not cancel EU carbon-border costs, and removing both UK instruments changes the competitive position of domestic producers.

What this depends on
  • Abolish the Emissions Trading Scheme: Removing the domestic carbon price changes the rationale for an import adjustment and the credit available under EU rules.
  • Cancelling investment has costs beyond this year’s budget: Energy investment and industrial support also affect manufacturers’ ability to compete.
Analysis and sources: analysis of Stop implementing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

Farmers and manufacturers using imported inputs have a legitimate interest in avoiding additional costs and emissions paperwork. Cancelling the UK’s planned charge would remove that prospective burden. However, its purpose is to address an imbalance between domestic production facing carbon costs and imports that may not. HMRC describes implementation from January 2027, including specified fertiliser imports. Design, reliefs and supplier responses determine the actual effect on input prices.

This is separate from the EU mechanism already operating from January 2026. Repealing the UK scheme would not remove the EU importer’s obligation on covered British exports. Abolishing the UK ETS alongside it may reduce deductible UK carbon payments and increase that commercial exposure. UK–EU ETS linkage could change the position, but negotiations do not guarantee an exemption. Reform needs a joined-up assessment of import users, domestic manufacturers, exporters and customers, rather than counting the removal of each UK charge as an independent benefit without its trade interactions. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Repudiate AR7 contracts and scrap AR8

Original measure 8 in Reindustrialising Britain. PDF p. 25

Costing uncertainty

What Reform proposes

Repudiate contracts for difference from allocation round 7 and cancel allocation round 8.

Repudiating awarded support risks legal costs and investor confidence, while removing contracts that can also return money when wholesale prices rise.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Repudiate AR7 contracts and scrap AR8

It is reasonable to scrutinise renewable support prices and the wider costs of connecting and balancing new generation. But rejecting existing contracts is a different decision from designing a better future auction. The government published round 7 results in January 2026, followed by round 7a results in February; those results describe contract offers, so the legal position must be checked project by project. Contracts for difference support a negotiated revenue level: generators receive top-ups when the reference price is lower, and pay back the difference when it is higher.

Treating them as an unconditional subsidy misses that protection. Repudiation could trigger disputes or compensation claims, depending on the executed contracts and legislation. It could also increase the return investors demand for future British infrastructure if government commitments look unreliable. Cancelling round 8 may avoid future commitments, but requires a replacement plan for capacity, finance and energy security. Any claimed saving should deduct legal exposure and replacement supply costs. [1] [2]

Implement the Fingleton Review immediately

Original measure 9 in Reindustrialising Britain. PDF p. 25

Reasonable policy with caveats

What Reform proposes

Implement the Fingleton Review's nuclear energy recommendations in full immediately.

Reducing unnecessary nuclear delays is useful, but implementation needs legislation, regulatory capacity and a deliverable construction and financing programme.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Implement the Fingleton Review immediately

The Fingleton review offers a serious route to reducing avoidable nuclear costs and delays. More predictable regulation could help bring reliable low-carbon electricity and skilled employment. This is therefore a proposal whose potential benefits should be acknowledged. Its claimed immediacy still needs testing. The government's March 2026 response says it had accepted the principles of all 47 recommendations and begun implementation, with necessary legislation intended before the end of 2027, subject to parliamentary timings. Reform should identify which remaining actions it would accelerate and how, rather than presenting the whole programme as untouched.

Changing guidance and legislation also requires trained regulators, competent project organisations and coordination across environmental and planning bodies. Even successful regulatory reform does not itself provide finance, construction teams or a completed reactor. The relevant test is a published delivery programme with responsibilities, milestones and safeguards. Consumers need an account of when electricity arrives and who carries construction risk, rather than a promise that an entire review can take effect at once. [1] [2]

Automatically approve French and South Korean nuclear designs

Original measure 10 in Reindustrialising Britain. PDF p. 25

Reasonable policy with caveats

What Reform proposes

Legislate to recognise French and South Korean nuclear designs automatically as safe.

Using trusted overseas assessments can save time, but a design's nationality cannot establish its suitability for a particular British site and operator.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Automatically approve French and South Korean nuclear designs

Reusing high-quality assessments from experienced overseas regulators could cut duplication and make established reactor designs easier to deploy. The benefit depends on the precise design version and evidence, rather than whether its vendor is French or South Korean. British nuclear approval separates generic design work from site licensing, environmental permits and planning decisions. Automatic recognition of a design would therefore leave practical questions about cooling, flooding, security, waste arrangements and the operator's capability. Those are matters for the actual project, not a foreign design certificate.

ONR's May 2026 update already describes a policy for using international regulators' judgements, so the useful comparison is with that developing approach. Parliament would need to know what additional checks the proposal removes and what replaces them. Faster assessment is worthwhile where evidence supports it; removing independent judgement by nationality could instead create uncertainty over responsibility. Financing, supply chains and construction performance would remain essential even after design recognition. [1]

Cut affordable housing on brownfield land

Original measure 11 in Getting homes built in cities. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Set affordable-housing requirements at 0% outside major cities and 5% in major cities for brownfield development.

A blanket cut can remove affordable homes without ensuring either cheaper land or faster building.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Cut affordable housing on brownfield land

Reducing contributions could make some difficult brownfield schemes viable, particularly where contamination or demolition makes redevelopment expensive. That is a real argument for examining costs. But a national zero-or-five-per-cent rule does not distinguish those sites from profitable developments that can support affordable homes. English viability guidance already provides a framework for considering abnormal costs and says policy obligations should be reflected in the value of land. Removing obligations therefore creates a distributional question: will the saving finance extra construction, or increase what purchasers can pay landowners?

There is no automatic requirement in this proposal for the saving to become lower sale prices. Households unable to buy market housing could lose a route to a secure home even if total completions rise. A credible assessment needs additional completions, lost affordable units and replacement subsidy considered together, with a clear definition of major cities. [1]

Expand permitted development on brownfield sites

Original measure 12 in Getting homes built in cities. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Introduce broad permitted development rights allowing brownfield land to become housing.

Faster permission needs enforceable housing quality and infrastructure safeguards to deliver places people can live well.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Expand permitted development on brownfield sites

A simpler route to permission can reduce uncertainty and bring redundant urban land back into use. The important question is what conditions accompany that route. Government-commissioned research examined permitted-development housing across 11 English areas using measures including space, amenity, location and design. It provides a reason to test living conditions rather than judge success only by the number of units approved. That historical study cannot establish the outcome of a future regime with different safeguards.

However, brownfield status alone says little about flood risk, contamination, daylight, school capacity or proximity to jobs. These issues still need decisions, funding and accountable enforcement even when a full planning application disappears. A broad entitlement could also make it harder to secure the right mix of homes locally. Reform needs to specify minimum standards, prior-approval checks and infrastructure contributions before claiming that deregulation will produce better housing outcomes. [1] [2]

Repeal biodiversity net gain

Original measure 13 in Getting homes built in cities. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Repeal Biodiversity Net Gain requirements for development.

Repeal removes a habitat-replacement mechanism, while the English system already has exemptions and flexible delivery routes.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Repeal biodiversity net gain

Biodiversity requirements can create survey costs, delays and particular difficulties for small builders. Those burdens deserve evidence and proportionate administration. But the English scheme is designed to leave measurable habitat improvements after development, rather than simply charge a fee for permission. In-scope developers can use onsite provision, offsite units and, as a last resort, statutory credits. Qualifying very small sites also gained an exemption in August 2026. It is therefore misleading to evaluate repeal as if every house faced the same unavoidable obligation.

Removing the scheme could reduce some private development costs, but somebody would then bear the loss of habitat or the cost of restoring it through another programme. Brownfield land can have ecological value too. A stronger case would identify which requirements prevent otherwise viable homes and compare targeted simplification with wholesale repeal, including the effect on habitat delivery and existing long-term restoration investment. Leave the Aarhus Convention

Original measure 14 in Getting homes built in cities. PDF p. 25

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Withdraw the UK from the Aarhus Convention.

Withdrawing from environmental participation rights does not establish that viable housing will be built faster.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Leave the Aarhus Convention

The convention covers access to environmental information, participation in decisions and access to independent review. Those rights help residents discover and contest the consequences of development, including pollution affecting their own neighbourhood. Reducing litigation uncertainty could benefit some projects, and repetitive or poorly founded challenges can consume public resources. But the case for withdrawal needs evidence about the delays specifically caused by convention obligations and the safeguards that would replace them. Leaving an international agreement would not, by itself, remove every domestic information duty, consultation procedure or ground of legal challenge.

Further legislative choices would determine the practical result. There is also a cost to weakening scrutiny: mistakes or omitted impacts may emerge only after construction, when remedies are expensive. This proposal reaches far beyond housing. Improving the quality and speed of decisions and court procedures should be compared with removing the rights themselves. [1]

Repeal and replace habitat protections

Original measure 15 in Getting homes built in cities. PDF p. 26

Distributional trade-off

What Reform proposes

Repeal and reform the Habitats Regulations, including Nutrient Neutrality requirements.

Ending the planning test leaves the extra pollution and the need to fund river restoration unresolved.

Analysis and sources: analysis of Repeal and replace habitat protections

Nutrient mitigation has delayed housing in affected English catchments, and government guidance acknowledges the additional cost. It is reasonable to ask whether collective treatment upgrades and catchment-wide solutions would work better than negotiating mitigation development by development. But neutrality addresses an underlying problem: additional wastewater can worsen pollution in already damaged protected waters. It is not a nationwide prohibition on housing, and the calculators were updated in March 2026. Repeal would remove a constraint on consenting development without necessarily removing the pollution it creates.

The plan needs to explain what replacement protection would apply, who would finance it and when effective capacity would be available. Habitat regulation also covers more than nutrient pollution, so broad repeal risks consequences beyond the stated housing problem. Faster permissions would be a benefit only if the replacement system credibly delivers both homes and functioning water and nature infrastructure. [1] [2]

The proposal needs to identify the legal restriction it intends to remove. Analysis and sources: analysis of Net tax reductions include offsetting revenues

A package can cut some taxes while raising charges or levies elsewhere. The relevant borrowing effect is the net change in public receipts, while the distributional effect depends on who pays and benefits. How this examination works

Each analysis examines the proposal on its own merits. The tests are practical: does the claimed mechanism work, who gains or loses, what does the evidence establish, and what remains uncertain?

We prefer UK public statistics, legislation, regulators, Parliament and published research. A cited source may establish the current rule or explain a mechanism without validating Reform’s costing. Historical findings are dated; projections, modelled estimates and our economic inferences are identified as such. England-only and devolved policies are distinguished where relevant.

Figures attributed to the plan remain attributed unless corroborated. The official plan and linked policy page publish headline costings but not the model needed to reproduce the results. The major-line disclosure audit records what is provided and what is missing. Exact proposal-level fiscal outcomes therefore remain unverified.
